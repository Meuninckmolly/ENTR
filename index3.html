01
01 · Age 23

A detour with no ceiling.

After graduating from Baylor, Neil had a job lined up at IBM. Before it started he had about six months to fill, so he began working alongside his father and figuring out the hotel business. He never took the IBM job.

What hooked him: no limit, no ceiling, just potential. Work hard, and you get to keep all of it.

“Whatever you get to create, and whatever you get to do, it's yours.”Neil Ratanji · Interview
Concept · Ownership & upside
  • Neil joked that he “was told to do this,” then laughed it off and gave the real answer: the thrill of unlimited potential.
  • He's candid that at 23 he wasn't thinking the way he does now at 42. It “wasn't nothing glorious.”
  • A salary can be stable, but it is capped by the role. Ownership trades that stability for upside, and the responsibility that comes with it.
  • His line: no one can take what you create from you “unless you give it to somebody.”
ReflectIf there were truly no ceiling, what would you be willing to build, and what would you be willing to give up for it?

02 · 2006 · College Station

Look at what's missing.

College Station is home to Texas A&M. When Neil's family bought their first location, he noticed there wasn't much there. What existed was dated and old.

His read of the market: it needed fresh product. He went on to build one location, then three more.

“The recognition was the need for fresh product.”Neil Ratanji · Interview
Concept · Opportunity recognition
  • An opportunity isn't just an idea. It's a gap between what customers can get and what they'd want, which you can fill better than others.
  • Neil's gap was quality. The market had hotels, but the product was out of date.
  • He describes the expansion as one location, then an additional three. That's the sequence from the interview, not a claim about his full portfolio today.
  • Local context matters: a university town creates steady demand and rare peak events (you'll see one in chapter 6).
ReflectThink of a place you know well. What feels “dated” there, and who would pay for something fresh?

03 · The early goal

The hill. Not the mountains.

Early on, Neil's goal was simple: get as many locations as possible. He didn't know the business would reach where it is today, and he admits that was very much a young guy's problem.

He calls it wearing horse blinders. You only see the hill you're climbing, not the mountains behind it.

“You don't really see past the hill that you're trying to get to.”Neil Ratanji · Interview (paraphrased closely)
Concept · Goal fixation
  • A single, narrow goal (“more locations”) can hide what it takes to sustain them: staffing, systems, debt, attention.
  • Neil's “mountains” are the harder problems that only appear after you reach the first hill.
  • Later he says he doesn't love goals as a framework at all, a hint that his thinking shifted from chasing a number to building something sustainable.

04 · Age 23–25

Hot-headed, and humbled.

Asked how he handled early obstacles, Neil was blunt: like a 23-to-25-year-old who thought he was the hottest thing since sliced bread. That attitude, he says, will really screw you up.

One early lesson came from buying a property, thinking he'd fix it up in six months and leave. It didn't work out that way. He failed, and he learned.

“Humility is a huge thing to have.”Neil Ratanji · Interview
Concept · Learning from failure
  • “You don't know how many people in your industry are miles ahead of you.” Some of the quietest people he meets are among the most accomplished.
  • His method for obstacles: when you find an older, experienced source, actively listen. No one reveals all their cards, but they'll give enough to build a solution.
  • He separates wealth from accomplishment. They aren't the same thing.
  • The failed fix-and-flip wasn't the end of the story. Chapter 6 shows what happened when he tried it again a decade later.
ReflectWho is “miles ahead” of you right now, and when did you last just listen to them?

  • No one shows you every card, so catch the clues.
  • Older, experienced voices have already made your mistakes.
  • Take notes mentally, then go build the solution.

05 · 19 years in business

Know who carries the load.

Looking back, Neil wishes he'd built better structure around staffing and learned sooner how to treat his employees. It took him a long time. He's been in business since 23, about 19 years.

His big idea: 80% of your staff does 20% of the work, and 20% does 80%. Digest that, he says, and “then you're in business.”

“I do give my 20% preferential treatment.”Neil Ratanji · Interview
Concept · Pareto thinking & team design
  • The point isn't that 80% are “worthless.” Neil says that's simply all they're going to give, and you have to be able to accept it.
  • Knowing your force helps you make better decisions about what the business can actually take on.
  • He says preferential treatment exists whether or not employers admit it, and he invests in the people who go above and beyond.
  • He credits those changes (structure, employees, investing in his 20%) with letting him live his life while the business still moves forward.
  • He mentions a Dallas experience where he “really figured it out,” but the transcript doesn't detail it.
ReflectA strong team is a design choice. How would you create roles and rewards so your top contributors stay?

06 · A decade later

Same play. Smarter player.

About ten years after the first flip didn't work, Neil bought another property, fixed it up and flipped it in around five and a half months. He did exactly what his younger self had imagined.

He wanted it open for a George Strait concert at Kyle Field, when rooms sold for about $1,500 a night. The person who had to sign off said it wouldn't happen in time. A week before the concert, Neil called him in. He signed.

“No one tells me I can't do something.”Neil Ratanji · Interview
Concept · Experience compounds
  • His point: there's no such thing as “old and slow.” Everything he'd learned carried forward and sped him up.
  • Event-driven demand is a real lever in a college town. A single weekend can price rooms far above normal.
  • Doubt as fuel: when someone says it can't be done, he treats it as a slap in the face, and he goes out to try.
  • Note the pattern: his drive didn't change, but his judgment did. Same flip, ten years of learning later.

07 · The value he delivers

A room is a room. Service is the difference.

Asked how his business creates value, Neil starts with a good product and good service. But when pressed for the main thing, it's service. A hotel room's a hotel room.

He still deals with guests face-to-face, and frontline feedback travels straight to him. It's about how you make them feel.

“A hotel room's a hotel room.”Neil Ratanji · Interview
Concept · Value proposition & segmentation
  • Neil sees a generational shift. Older guests (“boomers”) often buy the person: they want to know you, and trust you'll take care of them.
  • Many younger guests would rather check in at a kiosk, go to their room and check out without talking to anyone.
  • His answer isn't a single style. Know the demographic and background, then tailor. Read reviews and surveys, and train staff to treat each type of guest accordingly.
  • Technology, AI and social media keep changing expectations, so flexibility is the strategy.
  • His summary: it comes down to figuring out how to treat people.
ReflectGeneralizations like “boomer” and “younger guest” are Neil's shorthand. How would you check what each real customer actually wants?

08 · 2023

Still taking the risk.

As recently as 2023, Neil took on another project: an existing property in town that was headed to auction. He called the agent he knew and had it taken off the market, because the location was the best, with Kyle Field visible from the parking lot.

He didn't want the property's brand, so he shut it down, empty for two weeks, while the loan payment kept coming due. Then he gutted it, relaunched it as a Best Western, and it's now his number one location.

“The bank's not gonna sit back and wait.”Neil Ratanji · Interview (condensed)
Concept · Calculated risk
  • Risk didn't vanish with age. He had two boys and a wife at the time, and he still did it.
  • His lesson: a new life stage doesn't mean no risk. It means scaling the business so you can still live your life while you pursue it.
  • Notice the preparation: relationships (the agent), a clear thesis (location + a different brand), and a plan for the downtime.
  • Outcome as he reports it: the property became the top location in his portfolio.
ReflectWhat's the difference between a bold bet and a reckless one? Where did Neil put the guardrails?

09 · Pace

Low and slow beats the microwave.

Moving fast, aggressive, with multiple locations (“grow, grow, grow”) can actually hurt you. Neil's analogy: growth in a microwave boils over; smoked low and slow for a day and a half, it comes out amazing.

He's turned down many deals his agents brought him, including one he and Krish looked at and passed on immediately. You don't have to keep growing to make money.

“Go slow, but go wise.”Neil Ratanji · Interview
Concept · Sustainable growth
  • “Growth” doesn't have to mean property after property, or app release after app release. It's whatever your business is.
  • Saying no is a skill. A deal you don't take is a decision, and he says he learns from those too.
  • Too-fast growth can “come back to bite you,” because people and systems lag behind the footprint.
  • Compare with chapter 3: the young goal was “as many as possible.” The mature goal is “as many as I can run well.”
ReflectWhat is one opportunity you'd have to say no to in order to do your best work?

10 · Today · Age 42

Not stuck. Just free.

Nearly 20 years in, what keeps Neil going is the freedom and lifestyle the business gives him, time with his two boys and his wife, good money, and the people he's met. He wears shorts and a t-shirt to work.

He's not even sure he wants a different industry, but he knows he's not stuck in one. Life will happen, and you won't be a 23-year-old chasing it 16 hours a day forever.

“You're not stuck in one business.”Neil Ratanji · Interview
Concept · Designing the business around your life
  • He credits structure, employees and investing in his top 20% for letting the business go forward without consuming his life.
  • “Life isn't like that anymore,” he says of the 16-to-18-hour days of his early twenties, and he's glad he made the changes.
  • Neil's hot takes (his opinions): “Contracts are like hearts, they're meant to be broken,” but he's clear that breaking one costs money and you'd better be willing to pay it. Also: keep your bankers close. Treat this as a founder's provocative style, not legal advice. Read and understand what you sign.
  • Parting advice to the students: don't be afraid of the big move, but know the price of it.
